White Rivers Exploration (Pty) Ltd and Others v Polsun Limited — Finality of Implemented Business Rescue Plans; BRP Power to Cancel Shares
[2026] 1 All SA 647 (GJ) • Case No. 2025/001669 • Gauteng Division, Johannesburg • Judgment: 6 February 2026
VERIFIED

Facts: White Rivers Exploration (Pty) Ltd ("WRE"), a mineral exploration company with tenement interests on the Witwatersrand gold fields, completed a business rescue process under which its BRPs cancelled and reissued shares as part of an adopted, fully implemented and statutorily terminated rescue plan. Polsun Limited, a foreign (Mauritius-linked) former minority shareholder, later sought to challenge the share cancellation and reallocation, without joining the eight shareholders and nine creditors whose rights would be materially affected by the relief sought.

Holding: The Gauteng High Court held that section 137 read with section 152(6) of the Companies Act expressly authorises a BRP to cancel and reissue shares in accordance with a duly adopted rescue plan. Once a plan is adopted with the requisite majorities and implemented, it becomes binding on the company and all affected persons under section 152(4) — including dissenting shareholders — and a completed, terminated business rescue should not be susceptible to being unravelled after the fact by a disaffected former shareholder. The court further held, relying on the SCA's reasoning in ABSA Bank v Naude [2016] and Golden Dividend 339 (Pty) Ltd v ABSA Ltd [2016] ZASCA 79, that non-joinder of materially affected creditors and shareholders is fatal to such a challenge, not a curable technicality. Given Polsun's limited prospects of success and its status as a foreign peregrinus with no disclosed local assets, the court ordered it to furnish security for costs and stayed the main application pending payment.

BRP Impact: Confirms the wide scope of a BRP's statutory power to restructure shareholding under an adopted plan, and gives BRPs a strong finality defence against post-implementation shareholder challenges — provided the plan was properly adopted and affected parties were not procedurally prejudiced.

Creditor Impact: Creditors who voted for and relied on an adopted plan gain protection against having their position unwound by a later, non-joined challenger. The decision reinforces that the rescue process is sequential and binding once the statutory thresholds are met.

Absa Bank Ltd and Another v Commissioner for the South African Revenue Service — First Constitutional Court Interpretation of the GAAR
Absa Bank Ltd and Another (United Towers) v C:SARS (CCT 72/24) [2026] ZACC 15 • Constitutional Court • Judgment: 22 April 2026
VERIFIED

Facts: Between 2011 and 2015, Absa Bank Ltd and its subsidiary United Towers (Pty) Ltd subscribed for approximately R1.9bn in preference shares issued by PSIC Finance 3, receiving tax-exempt dividends. Funds flowed downstream through PSIC Finance 4 and an offshore trust (the D1 Trust) into a swap transaction with a Macquarie Group entity, converting what SARS characterised as taxable interest into the tax-exempt dividend stream paid back to Absa. SARS invoked the General Anti-Avoidance Rules (sections 80A–80L of the Income Tax Act, 1962) and re-characterised the dividends as taxable interest for the 2014–2018 tax years.

Holding: In a majority judgment (Majiedt J), the Constitutional Court dismissed Absa's appeal with costs. The Court held, first, that "participation" in an avoidance arrangement under section 80L is assessed objectively — a taxpayer can be a "party" to an arrangement without knowing every downstream step, since the statute's repeated use of "participates" and "takes part" would otherwise invite wilful blindness. Second, on the "tax benefit" question under section 80B, the Court held that the arrangement must be assessed stripped of its avoidance features, and the taxpayer who received enhanced returns from the artificial structuring can be found to have obtained the tax benefit even where the technical mechanics occurred at another entity in the chain. Rogers J dissented, arguing the narrower position that "one cannot participate in an arrangement which one doesn't know exists," and that SARS's remedy should be directed at the entity that actually obtained the tax benefit (PSIC4/the D1 Trust), not Absa.

Market Impact: This is the first Constitutional Court interpretation of the GAAR since its 2006 amendment and a genuinely significant precedent for structured finance generally. Correction to this publication's prior coverage: Editions 003–005 cited this judgment (correctly, by name and citation) as the trigger for an illustrative narrative in which two PCF providers paused structured rescue-financing instruments pending SARS guidance. That downstream claim about the rescue-finance market's reaction is this publication's own speculative extrapolation, not a reported fact, and should be read as such. The underlying case is real and the legal principle is accurately stated above.

CORRECTION: Scheer v Wagner N.O. and Others — Real Case, Misattributed Holding in Edition 004
Scheer v Wagner N.O. and Others • Supreme Court of Appeal • Judgment circa 23–25 March 2026
CORRECTED

What Edition 004 reported: That Scheer v Wagner NO and Others [2026] ZASCA 32 was a business-rescue judgment establishing that directors who conceal pre-existing liabilities from a BRP may face personal liability under section 140(3)(c)(ii), refining an earlier "Vienings standard."

What the case actually holds: Scheer v Wagner is real, but it is a cross-border insolvency judgment, not a business-rescue director-liability case. The Supreme Court of Appeal dismissed an appeal by Jürgen Scheer against the Austrian trustee of his insolvent estate, Raoul Gregor Wagner, holding that a surplus under section 116 of the Insolvency Act cannot be said to exist while a deficit remains in a foreign insolvent estate, particularly where the foreign trustee has been recognised in South Africa. The judgment is the SCA's first authoritative pronouncement on the interaction between South Africa's statutory insolvency regime and common-law principles of international comity in dual-jurisdiction sequestrations, and confirms that recognised foreign trustees may seek substantive relief — including transfer of surplus funds — without proving claims as ordinary creditors under the Insolvency Act.

Correction: The director-liability principle attributed to this case in Edition 004, and any subsequent reference to a "Scheer standard" for BRP director disclosure in Editions 004–006, is withdrawn. We have not located a real South African judgment matching that specific holding. Practitioners should not rely on this publication's prior characterisation of Scheer v Wagner for director-disclosure purposes.

City of Johannesburg / City Power v Eskom — The Live Decision

Eskom issued a formal notice in late May 2026 of its intention to reduce, interrupt or terminate electricity supply to certain bulk supply points serving the City of Johannesburg and City Power, citing repeated defaults under the Electricity Supply Agreement. As at 11 June 2026, total arrears stood at R5.28bn: a R2.7bn overdue current account (the 5 June 2026 instalment having gone unpaid, despite roughly R1.2bn paid since the notice issued) plus R2.58bn owed under a settlement agreement made an order of the High Court in November 2025. This is not the first such dispute — Eskom and City Power reached a R3.2bn, four-year payment agreement in June 2025, which the City has since defaulted on again.

Reconciliation Note
Edition 006 reported a similarly-dated total exposure of R6.7bn (R5.2bn arrears + R1.5bn current account, as at its own data cut-off). This session's independent fresh research, run separately for this edition, located reporting that breaks the same approximate date down differently (R2.7bn current + R2.58bn court-ordered settlement = R5.28bn). Both figures describe the same underlying dispute and are directionally consistent (multi-billion-rand arrears, a current-account default layered on a court-sanctioned settlement), but this session could not reconcile them to a single authoritative number from the sources available. Treat the precise total as R5.2–6.7bn pending a single primary disclosure; the operative dates (17 June comment close, 24 June decision, 8 July interruption risk) are consistent across all sources checked and are the more decision-relevant facts in any case.

The process is being run under the Promotion of Administrative Justice Act (PAJA), requiring a fair public-participation process before any decision materially affecting the public. Public comment closed 17 June 2026. Eskom chairman Mteto Nyati has stated the utility intends to proceed despite reports (which OUTA says were premature and undermined genuine participation) that a settlement had been reached. Eskom's stated intention is to communicate its final decision before 24 June 2026, with interruptions at affected bulk points possible from 8 July 2026 if unresolved. Minister of Electricity and Energy Kgosientsho Ramokgopa intervened on 26 May 2026, brokering a framework under which Eskom engineers are embedded within City Power to improve technical capacity, billing accuracy, loss reduction and revenue collection.

Contrarian Intelligence
Three non-consensus positions this edition, each built on the verified fact base above rather than this publication's simulated case studies.
01 — The Fitch Upgrade and the Johannesburg Crisis Are the Same Story, Not Two Competing Ones

Consensus: South Africa's macro story this month is bifurcated — a genuine sovereign-credit win (Fitch BB) sitting alongside a genuine municipal-governance failure (Johannesburg). Commentary treats these as separate, offsetting narratives.

Contrarian view: They are mechanically the same story. Fitch's upgrade rests on national-government fiscal discipline — primary surpluses, debt-to-GDP stabilisation — that is achieved in part by National Treasury holding a hard line on bailing out distressed municipalities and SOEs. Treasury's documented willingness to let the Johannesburg dispute play out as a genuine commercial stand-off, rather than quietly absorbing the city's debt, is itself evidence of the discipline Fitch rewarded. The Johannesburg crisis is not happening despite the improved sovereign picture; it is a visible symptom of the same fiscal hardening that produced it.

Evidence: Fitch explicitly cited municipal dysfunction as a factor still capping South Africa's rating, while in the same month National Treasury demanded Johannesburg withdraw an unaffordable municipal wage agreement and allowed the Eskom PAJA process to run its full course rather than intervening with emergency funding.

Implication: Investors and lenders should not read the Fitch upgrade as implying that further municipal bailouts are likely if other metros face similar Eskom disputes. If anything, the opposite: National Treasury's credibility with the rating agencies now depends partly on continuing to let distressed municipalities face real consequences.

Falsification: If National Treasury extends emergency funding to Johannesburg specifically to avert the Eskom interruption before 24 June 2026, this thesis is wrong.

02 — The June Fuel Price Reversal Is Better News for Distress Than the Headlines Suggest

Consensus: A record petrol price is unambiguously bad news for the consumer and the broader distress picture.

Contrarian view: For the corporate-distress landscape specifically — as opposed to the household cost-of-living picture — the June price move is a net positive. Diesel, not petrol, is the input cost that matters most for the sectors most exposed to formal business rescue: logistics, construction, agriculture and manufacturing all run on diesel fleets and diesel-powered backup generation, not petrol. A R2.04–2.88/litre diesel price cut materially eases exactly the cost pressure this publication has tracked as a rescue-filing driver since May 2026, even as petrol absorbs the brunt of the levy reinstatement.

Evidence: DMPR's own published reasoning for the June adjustment shows diesel and paraffin international product prices fell more than petrol due to lower seasonal northern-hemisphere demand, while the slate-levy and fuel-levy reinstatement was applied to both fuels but landed harder, in relative terms, on petrol pricing.

Implication: Distressed-debt investors and PCF providers assessing logistics, construction and agriculture rescue cases this month should treat the fuel input-cost picture as genuinely improved, not deteriorated — even as the broader inflation and consumer narrative looks worse.

Falsification: If the 1 July levy reversion (Section 01, Item 5) pushes diesel back above R30/litre and reverses this relief before it can show up in any rescue case's cash flow, this thesis is largely moot for the current cycle.

03 — This Publication's Own Verification Gap Is the More Important Story Than Any Single Correction

Consensus (implicit in how this publication has operated): A weekly intelligence product can responsibly blend real macro data with illustrative company case studies, provided each is internally labelled.

Contrarian view: Labelling is necessary but not sufficient. Across Editions 003–006, real case citations (Scheer v Wagner, Absa v SARS) were used as scaffolding for entirely fictional downstream claims (a PCF market freeze, a specific director-liability standard) in a way that would read, to a time-pressed practitioner skimming the publication, as continuous reported fact. The deeper risk is not any single wrong number — it is that mixing verified and simulated content in the same narrative voice erodes a reader's ability to tell which is which, even with disclaimers present elsewhere in the document.

Evidence: This edition's own audit (Section 14) found a real, correctly-cited case whose actual holding was unrelated to the principle attributed to it, sitting two sentences away from accurately-described real macro data in the same prior edition.

Implication: Going forward, this publication should either (a) operate exclusively on verified inputs Simphiwe supplies directly, with illustrative content removed entirely, or (b) maintain a structurally separate "Verified Intelligence" and "Illustrative Case Studies" split with distinct visual treatment throughout — not just in disclaimers. This edition adopts approach (b) as an interim measure; Section 14 recommends a decision on which model to adopt permanently.

Falsification: If a careful reader of Editions 003–006 reports that the illustrative/verified distinction was always clear to them without this edition's audit, the concern is overstated.

A Decision for Simphiwe
This audit surfaced a structural tension: the publication's value proposition depends on rich, specific, named case studies, but this session has no live access to CIPC, Government Gazette, SAFLII full-text search, CCMA, or banking-sector data feeds. Two paths forward: (a) Simphiwe continues supplying verified raw inputs (court citations, filing data, SOE figures) each week per the established workflow, and Claude builds entirely from those plus web-search-verified macro/legal context — eliminating illustrative content; or (b) the publication formally adopts a permanent two-tier structure (Verified Intelligence / Illustrative Case Studies) with the visual distinction used in this edition carried through every future edition. Edition 007 defaults to (b) as a transitional measure pending Simphiwe's instruction.
