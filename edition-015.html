Section 01
Executive Intelligence Summary
The verified record for 22 August–11 October 2026 shows a macro squeeze arriving in sequence: the SARB hiked the repo rate 25bp to 7.25% (prime 10.75%) in September; Stats SA reported Q2 GDP contracting 0.2% q/q and official unemployment rising to 33.6%; and the 7 October DMPR adjustment added R2.84–3.33/litre across fuels. Against this, company liquidation counts kept falling (July 239, August 227), and the City of Johannesburg's R5.25bn Eskom settlement of 21 August is now corroborated by six outlets. On the legal side, the Supreme Court of Appeal handed down NUMSA v SCAW South Africa on 29 September, a retrenchment-severance ruling listed on SAFLII. No CIPC or Gazette data was supplied, so company-level sections are Data Gap Notices.

Section 12
Contrarian Intelligence
01 — Falling Liquidation Counts Are a Lagging Signal, Not Evidence of Easing Distress

Consensus: Liquidations of 239 (July) and 227 (August), down 23% and 15% year-on-year, show corporate distress easing.

Contrarian view: The count lags the real economy. In the same window GDP contracted 0.2%, unemployment rose 0.9 points to 33.6%, the repo rate rose to 7.25% and diesel rose over R2.80/litre. Stats SA's own series was also restated upward by 75% for Q1 (Ed.012), showing the counts are unstable.

Evidence: Stats SA liquidations, GDP and QLFS releases cited in Sections 01, 04 and 08. Implication [ANALYTICAL]: do not read the counts as a turn; look at Q4 data.

Falsification: If September and October liquidations stay below 230 per month while the October fuel and September rate increases feed through, this view is wrong.

02 — The SARB Hiked Into a Contraction Because the Shock Is Supply-Side, Not Because Demand Is Strong

Consensus: Raising rates after a GDP contraction is a policy error.

Contrarian view: Q2 weakness was concentrated in manufacturing, mining and trade and in net exports (about −1.1 pp), while household consumption rose 0.4% and construction and agriculture grew. Coverage reports a unanimous MPC citing the fuel shock. The rate hike therefore targets inflation expectations from imported fuel costs, not a demand boom, and will bite hardest on leveraged firms in the sectors already contracting.

Evidence: Stats SA Q2 GDP breakdown (8 Sept); SARB decision coverage (Sept). Implication [ANALYTICAL]: expect rate-hike stress to appear first in mining, manufacturing and trade rescue plans.

Falsification: If Q3 household consumption contracts, the demand-resilience premise is wrong.
