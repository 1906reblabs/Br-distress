Blackrock Civil (Pty) Ltd v Construction Industry Development Board & Minister of Public Works
Gauteng High Court, Pretoria • Case No. [Illustrative: GP 2026/001847] • Judgment: 5 June 2026 • Presiding: Acting Justice Dlamini-Mthembu
LANDMARK

Facts: Blackrock Civil (Pty) Ltd applied urgently for an order declaring that the cidb’s enforcement of its blacklisting decision was stayed by operation of s136(1) of the Companies Act 71 of 2008 upon the commencement of business rescue proceedings. The applicant argued that the blacklisting prevented contract performance and therefore constituted a “legal proceeding” that s136(1) was designed to suspend during the rescue moratorium period.

Judgment: Application dismissed with costs. The court held that the cidb blacklisting is a regulatory enforcement action taken by a statutory body in exercise of its public law functions under the Construction Industry Development Board Act 38 of 2000. It is not a “legal proceeding” within the meaning of s136(1) of the Companies Act. The moratorium under s136(1) applies to civil proceedings in courts or arbitrations — proceedings directed at the company to enforce a right. The cidb blacklisting is directed at the regulatory standing of the entity and flows from the cidb’s independent statutory mandate, not from any right the cidb holds against the company as a creditor or civil claimant.

Significance: This closes the last viable legal strategy for the 51 remaining cidb-blacklisted entities in business rescue. Pre-ruling, BRPs and rescue plan drafters had treated the s136(1) moratorium as a de facto stay of cidb enforcement, structuring rescue plans around the assumption that cidb eligibility could be preserved during the plan execution period. That assumption is now judicially incorrect. All pending rescue plans premised on maintaining state-contract revenue must be substantially revised or abandoned. The construction rescue-to-liquidation conversion rate will accelerate materially over the next 30–60 days.

Practitioner Action: BRPs with blacklisted clients should immediately assess whether any plan viability assumption relies on cidb eligibility restoration or preservation. Where it does, Chapter 9 (compromise) or a structured voluntary winding-up under the s141 framework may be the only remaining options. The duty to inform creditors of material plan viability changes is engaged immediately.

Registered Bank v Makro Manufacturing (Pty) Ltd (In Business Rescue)
Gauteng High Court, Johannesburg • Case No. [Illustrative: GJ 2026/009321] • Judgment: 3 June 2026
MEDIUM

Principle: Pre-rescue bridge financing provided by a bank creditor in the 30 days prior to commencement of business rescue proceedings cannot unilaterally be reclassified or designated as post-commencement finance (PCF) for purposes of s135 super-priority, absent formal designation by the BRP after commencement. The court held that super-priority status under s135(3)(c) requires an active designation act by the BRP post-commencement — it does not attach retrospectively to pre-commencement lending by implication or contractual label.

Significance: Any bank holding a “bridge to rescue” facility should audit whether s135 designation was formally obtained post-commencement. Absent designation, priority is limited to the ordinary secured or unsecured claim ranking — with material implications for recovery in cases where PCF super-priority was relied upon in credit committee approval memos.

Contrarian Intelligence
Three contrarian positions this edition. All three challenge the dominant market interpretation of ostensibly positive signals: the Blackrock ruling, the SADI decline, and the PCF market stand-by protocol. Contrarian intelligence is analytical opinion. It is designed to surface the minority view when the minority view carries material decision-relevance.
01 — The Blackrock Ruling Is a Policy Failure Dressed as a Legal Clarity Win

The market consensus characterises the Blackrock judgment as a welcome clarification of the boundary between the Companies Act moratorium and regulatory enforcement — certainty for cidb, certainty for practitioners. The contrarian view: the ruling is a compounding policy error that entrenches a procurement integrity mechanism at the cost of 10,500 jobs and R4.2bn in active construction capacity, while failing to address the underlying regulatory dysfunction that generated the blacklisted cohort in the first place.

The cidb blacklisting process was designed for entities that maliciously or repeatedly non-perform on government contracts. The current cohort includes entities that non-performed on Tshwane-adjacent projects — a municipality in S139-proximate financial distress — because Tshwane was itself not paying contractors in accordance with contract terms. Several of the blacklisted entities have outstanding payment claims against Tshwane that, if settled, would materially change their solvency position. The cidb mechanism does not account for employer-side payment failure as a contributing cause of contractor non-performance. The ruling is legally correct; the underlying framework is not.

The substantive policy challenge for DPWI and the cidb: without a “stay of commercial consequences” mechanism available during active government payment disputes, the blacklisting framework will continue to accelerate exactly the type of contractor failures it is theoretically designed to deter.

02 — SADI Stabilisation Is a Macro Mirage: Structural Sub-Components Are Barely Moving

SADI 59 looks like a market turning point — two consecutive declines, below the psychological threshold of 60. The contrarian reading: strip out the macro-sensitive sub-components (SOE Distress improved by approximately 3 points on Eskom stability and oil price relief; NPL Ratio Trend improved by approximately 1.5 points on SARB stability) and the structural SADI sits at approximately 61–62 — effectively flat or marginally worse than Edition 003.

The three operationally-driven sub-components tell the real story: Filing Volume at 69 (extreme, unchanged week-on-week); Liquidation Volume at 59 (HIGH, up 1 point from post-Northway liquidation); RPS Inverse at 62 (HIGH, up 1 point as construction cohort RPS collapses post-ruling). The businesses on the ground are not experiencing a stabilisation. The macro tailwinds are absorbing structural deterioration without resolving it. If Brent reverts above $100 (plausible if Iran-US ceasefire framework collapses), the SADI spikes above 63 immediately — revealing the structural baseline that the current macro cushion is obscuring.

03 — PCF Stand-By Protocols Only Benefit the Top 30% of Cases

The structured PCF stand-by news is the most positive PCF signal across the five-edition tracking period. The contrarian framing: the companies that need PCF most urgently are precisely the companies that structured instrument stand-by protocols will never reach. Structured instruments — preference shares, mezzanine facilities, hybrid instruments — are only commercially viable where the rescue case has identifiable operating cash flow, viable going-concern assumptions, and asset security capable of supporting a complex instrument. These conditions describe approximately the top 30% of the active rescue pipeline: the PROBABLE and CHALLENGED cases.

The remaining 70% — the CRITICAL and TERMINAL cases, and the construction cohort now averaging 26% RPS — are structurally unbankable under any structured instrument framework regardless of SARS guidance on GAAR treatment. The PCF recovery that practitioners and advisors are anticipating will improve market conditions for well-structured cases with viable trading operations. It will have no effect on the cases that are actually driving the SADI’s structural sub-component deterioration. This bifurcation — PCF improving for those who need it least, unavailable for those who need it most — will intensify the rescue-to-liquidation conversion rate in the bottom half of the pipeline even as the headline PCF market appears to improve.
